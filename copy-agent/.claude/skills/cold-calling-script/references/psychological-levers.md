# Psychological Levers Reference

Source: Dylan's "Cold Calling Script V.1 Construction" doc (the original theoretical seed for the script lineage). 27 named cognitive biases/persuasion levers, each with a definition and a cold-call-specific example. Not all of these are used in the current script — most aren't. This is a reference shelf to pull from when a beat needs a new angle, not a checklist to satisfy.

1. **Inconsistency avoidance tendency** — people resist acting against their prior stated identity/decision. *Getting a prospect to actively schedule and reminding them of that decision makes a no-show feel inconsistent with who they said they'd be.*
2. **Curiosity tendency** — people hate an incomplete picture. *Over-explaining your offer upfront saps the curiosity that would otherwise drive them to book a call.*
3. **Reciprocation tendency** — people feel obligated to return a favor. *Offering a flexible payment plan "because I like you" invites the prospect to reciprocate by agreeing.*
4. **Status quo bias** — people default to trusting existing methods. *Words like "traditional" and "tried and tested" signal safety without sounding generic.*
5. **Rhyme as reason effect** — rhyming claims feel more true. *"We make client acquisition easy" lands better than an equivalent non-rhyming line.*
6. **Subjective validation** — claims that mirror someone's personal experience feel more true. *Naming the prospect's exact pain pattern makes them more likely to act on it.*
7. **Reason respecting tendency** — people comply more when given any reason, even a weak one. *Adding "because ___" after a call to action improves response.*
8. **Zero risk bias** — people overvalue eliminating risk entirely, even over a better risk/reward ratio. *Frame working with you as the zero-risk option vs. the "risk" of the status quo.*
9. **Humour bias** — people act on things that amuse them. *A well-placed joke, especially in objection handling, can shift the conversation.*
10. **Permission-based framing** — asking permission to ask creates a sense of control and raises compliance. *Opening with "do you mind if I take 30 seconds" outperforms diving straight in.*
11. **Frame control** — emotional states are contagious; whoever holds their frame most persistently wins the exchange. *Never match a doubtful prospect's frame — hold confident frame until they join it.*
12. **Disarming honesty bias** — radical transparency builds trust fast. State a limitation, then pivot with "but." *"I can't get you 150 appointments a month — but if 20-30 is what you need, that I can do."*
13. **Tried n' tested bias** — claims of age/proven pedigree increase trust. *"A system tested and refined over X months" reads as more credible than "new."*
14. **Politeness bias** — basic courtesy (please/thank you) measurably improves response.
15. **Kantian fairness tendency** — people are sensitive to perceived unfairness, especially toward themselves. *"You work this hard and still don't have consistent bookings — that's not fair, and it's exactly what we fix."*
16. **Ben Franklin effect** — people who've done you a favor like you more and are primed to do further favors. *Asking a small favor early (confirm an email address) builds goodwill before the ask.*
17. **Ambiguity effect** — people avoid acting on unclear information. *Keep every claim and next step unambiguous.*
18. **Urgency bias** — perceived time pressure drives action. *"This won't work as well once [platform] changes X — worth moving on now."*
19. **Hyperbolic discounting** — people favor smaller immediate rewards over larger delayed ones. *Frame the offer around a fast first win, not just the long-term payoff.*
20. **Ostrich effect** — people avoid confronting problems they don't want to face. *Position your service as the thing that lets them stop worrying about it.*
21. **Persistence effect** — the action threshold drops the more persistently a request is repeated. *Never treat a follow-up as "the last one" — persistence is the weapon, not a liability.*
22. **Originality bias** — original stimuli (not sales mechanics) get more response. *An original outreach format cuts through where a copied one doesn't.*
23. **Creativity bias** — creative stimuli outperform generic ones for the same reason as originality.
24. **Conviction bias** — statements delivered with confidence are more believable regardless of actual validity. *"This will completely change your life," said with real conviction, lands.*
25. **Dramaqueen bias (hyperbole)** — mild exaggeration of both the problem and the confidence in the solution drives action, used carefully.
26. **Disqualified identity bias** — explicitly stating who your offer *isn't* for makes the right-fit prospect self-select in. *"This isn't for people chasing a quick fix — it's for people committed long-term."*
27. **Hard work bias** — business owners overestimate their own work ethic; naming and honoring that is leverageable. *"I know you're a hard worker, and that's exactly why I want to work with you."*
28. **Trend bias (don't get left behind)** — fear of missing a rising trend drives action. *"We're early in a trend I want to get you in on before everyone else catches up."*

Two more appear applied directly in the earliest full script draft, worth keeping alongside the above:
- **Subjective validation via pain mirroring** — naming the exact operational bind ("buried in admin, no time to coach — or the reverse") gets a prospect nodding along before the pitch even starts.
- **Zero risk bias via guarantee framing** — "pay by performance, or you don't pay a thing" is the clearest zero-risk-bias application in the corpus and is worth reusing whenever a genuine performance guarantee is available.
