# No-Show Psychology — Reference

Baseline captured 2026-08-26 from Dylan's research (Cialdini commitment/consistency literature,
healthcare no-show studies, behavioral-economic SMS redesign studies). Read this before drafting or
revising any no-show recovery sequence — it explains *why* a no-show happens, which is what should
drive the copy, not just "someone missed a call, be nice about it."

## What's actually happening when a prospect no-shows

**1. The commitment was never strong enough to hold.** Booking by clicking a link is passive,
low-effort, and private. Cialdini's commitment/consistency research shows the commitments that
predict follow-through are active, public, effortful, and freely chosen — a link click has none of
those properties, so there's nothing pulling the prospect back once their attention moves elsewhere.
A restaurant cut no-shows meaningfully just by changing "please call if you have to change your
plans" to "*will you* please call if you have to change your plans?" and waiting for the customer to
actually say yes — same appointment, but now with a verbal commitment attached.

**2. This is measured, not theoretical, in the appointment literature.** Healthcare no-show research
is the closest real-world analog to a booked-call funnel — same problem, someone books then doesn't
show. A pilot combining social-norm signage with active commitment (patients writing down their own
appointment time and verbally committing to it) cut missed appointments by 31.7% vs. baseline, with
the effect disappearing when the intervention was paused and returning when reinstated. That's a
commitment-strength effect, not a reminder effect.

**3. Generic reminders barely move the number; specific framing does.** Stating the specific cost of
missing an appointment produced an 8.2% no-show rate vs. 9.9% for a generic cost message. Separately,
redesigning SMS wording around behavioral-economic principles cut hospital no-show rates by over
30%. A vague "see you soon!" and a specific, loss-framed reminder look similar but are not the same
intervention.

**4. Temporal discounting kills urgency between booking and call time.** The pain that made them
book is vivid at the moment of booking and decays the longer the gap runs. By the time the call
arrives, the felt cost of missing it is lower than the felt cost of sitting through an unplanned
sales conversation — standard hyperbolic discounting.

**5. Post-booking dissonance, not disinterest, drives a lot of ghosting.** A no-show isn't
necessarily someone who decided the offer isn't worth their time — often it's someone who agreed in
the moment, then had second thoughts alone with no one to talk them back down. Avoidance (silence)
is the path of least resistance out of that discomfort, easier than the awkward "actually I've
changed my mind" conversation.

## What this means for the recovery sequence

- **Don't reward avoidance with silence, and don't punish it with guilt.** Shame triggers more
  avoidance, not less. The sequence has to make reappearing low-cost, not confessional.
- **Re-insert a specific, concrete loss, not a vague "we missed you."** Generic framing
  underperforms specific framing by a wide margin (§3 above) — name the actual thing they lose by
  staying no-showed (the specific gap/problem the offer solves), not just "let's reschedule."
- **Rebuild a real commitment on the reschedule, not just a new time slot.** Get a small active step
  — a reply, a one-line confirm, picking their own time and stating it back — rather than another
  passive link-only ask. A link with nothing attached to it is the same weak commitment that failed
  the first time.
- **Move fast.** The first touch should land same-day, before the dissonance hardens into a settled
  "not interested" story.
- **Reactivate the original pain, don't just apologize for the miss.** The thing that got them to
  book is the thing that gets them back, and it's already decaying (temporal discounting, §4) — every
  touch should do some work to re-surface that pain, not just process the logistics of the miss.
